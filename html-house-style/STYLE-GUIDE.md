# House style for HTML pages

This guide says how to build an HTML page that a demanding reader will trust. It is opinionated on purpose. Where it says "never", it means never unless the reader asks. Where it gives a number, use the number.

The rules come from long feedback on generated pages, diagrams, decks and dashboards: what readers kept, what they sent back, and why. Each section says what to do first and the reason after it, so you can judge cases this guide does not cover.

Use it with the files beside it:

- `PATTERNS.md`: the six page patterns. Choose one by the reader's job before using anything in this guide; it sets the page's structure, navigation, state and print behaviour.
- `examples/`: one working page per pattern.
- `base.css`: the stylesheet. Inline all of it into every page you generate.
- `template.html`: a decision brief, then a component gallery that shows every component in `base.css` once. Copy the brief's structure; delete the gallery.
- `CHECKLIST.md`: the short version to run before you hand anything over.
- `check.mjs`: the static checks from the checklist as a script with no dependencies.

**Precedence.** The reader's explicit request wins. Next comes the host project's own design system or framework: inside a codebase that has its own CSS and components, apply the writing and structure rules and leave its styling alone unless asked. This guide comes last. A page generated by code in the repository is changed at its generator, never in the emitted HTML. Inside a host that has its own tests and specs, those replace `check.mjs` and this kit's structure rules; apply only the writing rules, and only where the host has none. A multi-page or multi-view app with routes is out of scope, even when it ships as one generated file.

## 1. The reader

The kit is tuned for one kind of reader: a senior technical leader who is short on time and long on context. Edit this section if yours differs; the rest of the guide follows from it.

- **They read for the decision first.** They want the answer, the cost and the reason to stop on the first screen, then the evidence in order below it. Background research and method are useful but secondary. Treat them as an annex.
- **They come back.** Most pages are read more than once: before a meeting, during it, a week later. Stable section names, a contents rail and deep links make return visits fast.
- **They think in structure.** Named frameworks, layered decomposition, sequences ("first this, then this"), and where something came from before where it is going. A pile of facts with no shape reads as unfinished.
- **They are visual and distrust decoration.** A good diagram, schematic map, photo or timeline that shows the thing at a glance is welcome. Anything there only to look nice is noise.
- **They notice craft.** Misaligned boxes, cards that touch, labels on top of lines, a heading that leaves one word on its last line, a diagram they cannot zoom: each one costs the page credibility.
- **They are not a specialist in every field the page covers.** Assume a capable engineer or executive from outside the topic. Every term of art gets a plain definition the first time it appears.
- **Mostly laptop, sometimes phone.** Documents they read over must work on a phone with no sideways scrolling. Tools and dashboards can be desktop-first but must hold together on a phone.

## 2. The ten rules

1. **Answer first.** A page that argues or reports opens with one bottom-line block: the recommendation or finding, why now, what it costs, what would change the answer. Reference and tool pages open with one sentence of purpose.
2. **Headings are claims.** "CI time is now the bottleneck", never "Background". A reader skimming only headings should get the argument. Navigation sections at the end are the exception.
3. **Restraint.** Warm neutral ground, near-black text, one accent colour, a second accent used rarely. Colour carries meaning.
4. **Serif for reading, sans for interface, mono for data.** Body 18px at 1.62 line height, measure `64ch` (about 70 to 75 characters).
5. **Space instead of boxes.** Separate sections with whitespace and hairlines. Use a card only for things that form a group.
6. **Every visual earns its place.** If removing it would not hurt understanding, remove it.
7. **Diagrams are legible at the size they are read, and can be enlarged.** Every label at least 12px on screen.
8. **Plain words.** Define terms where they first appear, spell out acronyms, never use internal codes as names in prose.
9. **One self-contained file.** Inline CSS and vanilla JavaScript. No CDNs, frameworks, web fonts or external requests.
10. **Check before you say it is done, and say how you checked.** Render it if you can. If you cannot, run the static checks and say so.

## 3. Information architecture

Start with `PATTERNS.md`. Name what the reader will do in the minute after they open the page (decide, understand, look up, monitor, do or review); the pattern for that job sets the opening, the order of sections and the navigation. The rules in this section apply inside every pattern. Where a pattern names a different opening or skeleton, the pattern wins, because it is more specific to the reader's job.

### 3.1 Open with the bottom line, when there is one

The first screen holds, in this order: a small kicker naming the document type, a title that states the main claim, a one or two sentence dek, a meta line (version, date, reading time at about 230 words a minute), then the opening block.

- **A page that argues or reports a finding** opens with `.bluf`, three to five lines. For a decision: what to approve, why now, cost, stop conditions. For research: the finding, how confident, what it means for the reader. For status: on track or not, the one thing that needs attention, what happens next.
- **A reference, tool or log page** (a glossary, a lookup table, a calculator, a packing list) has no conclusion. Do not invent one. Open with one `.purpose` sentence saying what the page is for and how to use it.
- **A dashboard** puts one computed status sentence in `.bluf`, with no list: "On track: 2 of 3 milestones met; the API migration is blocked on access approval."

Reason: the reader decides in the first thirty seconds whether to keep reading. This is Minto's answer-first pyramid, and the dashboard version of Shneiderman's "overview first, then details on demand".

### 3.2 Order the body as an argument

After the bottom line, the body runs: the situation (what is true now, with numbers), the complication (what changed), the recommendation or model, the options considered (recommended one marked), the plan (as steps), the risks and stop conditions, then detail for the people doing the work. Situation, complication, then answer is Minto's order too. Glossary, method and sources go last.

When the page explains how something changed, use: where it came from, where it is now, where it is going. When it teaches how something works, give the big picture first and the concepts in the order they depend on each other, with any history after the model (PATTERNS.md, Understand).

### 3.3 Argument headings state claims; navigation headings are labels

Every argument `h2` is a sentence a reader could disagree with: "One cache service sits between runners and storage", "A shared service beats bigger runners on hit rate and cost". Topic labels ("Architecture", "Options", "Next steps") are not allowed there. `h3` may be shorter but still says something.

Navigation sections (Glossary, Sources, Method, Appendix, Detail) keep plain labels, sit after the argument, and carry `<h2 class="annex">`. The rail shows them after a divider (`li.annex-start`).

Reason: the contents rail lists the `h2`s. A rail of claims is a summary of the page; a rail of topic labels is a filing system.

### 3.4 Three depths, one argument

Long material serves three readers at once: the person who decides, the people who back and staff the work, and the people who build it.

- The **top** of the page (or a separate short brief) carries the whole argument in two printed pages or less.
- The **body** carries the reasoning, the options, the plan.
- **Collapsible detail and appendices** carry the evidence, the method, the tables and the edge cases.

Each depth tells the reader where the next one down is. Never make the decider wade through builder detail to find the ask.

### 3.5 Progressive disclosure

Put detail behind `<details>` with a summary line that says what is inside ("How cache keys are computed", "Cost breakdown"). Use tabs only when the reader picks one of several parallel views (the same plan by week or by team). Never hide the main argument, the ask or the risks behind a click. Collapsed sections print open; `base.css` and the template script both handle it.

### 3.6 Sequences look like sequences

Anything ordered in time (a plan, a process, a migration) uses numbered steps (`ol.steps`), each with a bold first line saying what happens and when, then one or two sentences. Dated events use `ol.timeline`. A bulleted list of phases loses the order.

### 3.7 Terms get a definition the reader can reach without leaving

Wrap each specialist term in `<span class="term" tabindex="0" data-def="...">` on first use. Hovering or focusing shows the definition: under the term on wide screens, pinned to the bottom of the screen on phones. Also list every term in a glossary at the end (`dl.glossary`), and mark every glossary entry as a `.term` where it first appears.

### 3.8 Navigation and the way back

- Add the contents rail (`.with-toc` with `nav.toc`) when the page has four or more `h2`s or about 1,200 words or more. Otherwise leave it out.
- Write the rail's list into the HTML yourself, so it works with JavaScript off and in a static check. The script only marks the current section.
- Markup order inside `.with-toc` is `.bluf`, then `nav.toc`, then `.prose`. On a phone the reader sees the bottom line first and the rail is a collapsed box under it; on wide screens the rail sits in its own column.
- Every `h2` has an `id`, so any section can be linked.
- In multi-view tools, every object (a component, a finding, a person, a work item) has the same shape everywhere it appears: same card, same fields in the same order, same link to its detail, same way back.

### 3.9 Research and method are an annex

The research trail, rejected drafts and deliberation notes are useful context. Put them last, as an appendix or a collapsed section, written up to be readable.

## 4. Writing on the page

The page's prose matters as much as its styling. A good-looking page full of jargon still fails.

- **Plain words.** Use the vocabulary the reader's field already uses. Avoid process-improvement jargon and team-internal shorthand.
- **Define on first use, in a clause:** "a cold runner, one that starts with an empty cache".
- **Spell out acronyms on first use;** drop an acronym that appears only once.
- **Name things by what they are.** Internal codes (ticket numbers, rule numbers, claim ids, phase codes) never stand in for the thing in prose. Write "the rollback rule (R2)" at most. Codes are fine in tables and appendices.
- **Restructure a sentence that needs a second read.** Subject and verb early; one example beats a paragraph of principle.
- **No filler:** no stacked contrast constructions, no rhetorical questions, no lists of three when there are two real items, no closing sentence that restates the paragraph, no "it's worth noting". Self-check questions in a learning page are fine.
- **No em dashes.** Use commas, colons, parentheses or a new sentence.
- **Numbers.** Every measured or external figure (a metric, a price, a market number, a survey result) names its source and date, in the text, the caption, the stat tile or the table note. Plan parameters, counts of things on the page and your own estimates do not need a source, but estimates say "estimate". Round in the direction that does not flatter the recommendation: benefits down, costs and risks up. Keep the exact figure in a table.
- **Separate measured from assumed** in the sentence itself: "the logs show" against "we expect".

## 5. Visual language

### 5.1 The feel

A well-made book crossed with a good engineering tool: quiet colour, generous whitespace, strong typographic hierarchy, almost no ornament. When unsure whether to add something, leave it out.

### 5.2 Colour

Colour comes from tokens in `base.css`. Never write a colour value in page CSS, a `style` attribute or an SVG attribute; use the role tokens (`--fg`, `--accent`, `--hairline` and so on).

- **Ground:** warm off-white page (`--bg`), slightly lighter surfaces for cards and figures (`--surface`), slightly darker sunken areas for code and table headers (`--sunken`).
- **Text:** near-black (`--fg`), muted grey for secondary text (`--fg-muted`), lighter grey only for metadata (`--fg-subtle`). All three pass WCAG AA (4.5:1) on `--bg`, `--surface` and `--sunken`. Do not put `--fg-subtle` text or bordered controls on tinted callout grounds; some palettes fall just under the target there.
- **One accent** (`--accent`) for links, the active contents item, the recommended option, the focus of a diagram and the bottom-line rule.
- **A second accent** (`--accent-2`) used rarely: the kicker, the ornament, a decision callout. If it shows up more than three or four times on a screen, it is overused.
- **Status colours** (ok, warn, danger) only mean status. Status is always a shape plus a word (`.status.ok`, `.status.warn`, `.status.danger`, `.status.idle`), because red and amber look alike to many readers.
- **Categories** (zones, teams, series, actor types) use `--cat-1` to `--cat-6`. They are tested for contrast in light and dark and for separation under the three common colour-vision deficiencies. The same category keeps the same colour everywhere on the page, always with a legend or a direct label. About six is the practical limit; past six, group or reuse a hue with a pattern.
- **Never:** decorative gradients (the ornament's dot pattern is the one exception), neon, purple-to-blue "AI" gradients, rainbow palettes, coloured section backgrounds behind body text, more than two accents.

**Palettes.** `base.css` ships three. Set one on the `html` element with `data-palette`:

| Palette | Feel | Use for |
|---|---|---|
| `paper` (default) | Warm editorial: paper ground, ink text, deep blue, clay | Briefs, reports, explainers, anything read at length |
| `slate` | Cool neutral, blue accent | The most conservative corporate audience |
| `graphite` | Near-monochrome, teal accent | Dense tools and dashboards; austere pages |

To make a new palette, copy one `:root[data-palette=...]` block and change the ground (`--p-bg`, `--p-surface`, `--p-sunken`), the accent pair (`--p-accent`, `--p-accent2`) and their tints. Keep text and border values unless you re-check contrast: body text at least 4.5:1 against every ground (aim for 7:1), control borders and meaningful graphics at least 3:1. Add a matching dark accent.

**Dark mode.** Every page follows the reader's system setting and also works when forced with `data-theme="light"` or `"dark"`. Dark mode is the same layout on a dark neutral ground with lightened accents. Printing always uses the light values.

### 5.3 Typography

The stacks in `base.css` use local fonts only. They resolve to good matches on macOS (Iowan Old Style, Palatino, Menlo) and Windows (Sitka, Cambria, Georgia, Cascadia Code, Consolas). On Linux and ChromeOS they fall back to the system serif, which reads well but looks plainer. If you know the page will be read there, the UI sans is an acceptable body face.

- **Reading text:** a warm serif (`--font-read`), 18px, line height 1.62, measure `64ch`. On phones, 17px. The empty right margin on a wide screen is intended; do not widen prose to fill it.
- **Headings:** a serif display face (`--font-display`) at weight 600, sentence case. Hierarchy comes from size and weight. `h3` is always larger than body text and `h4` never smaller.
- **Interface text:** labels, captions, tables, buttons, metadata and the contents rail use the system sans (`--font-ui`) at 14px.
- **Data and code:** the system monospace (`--font-mono`). Numbers in tables and stat tiles use tabular figures.
- **Uppercase labels** with wide tracking, at 12px, only for kickers and block labels ("BOTTOM LINE"). Never for headings or sentences.
- **Scale:** reading sizes step by 1.2 from an 18px base; interface sizes are 14 and 12. Use only the `--fs-*` tokens.
- **Emphasis:** bold (650) for the lead-in of a list item or a key phrase, italic for titles and quotes. Underlines are links.
- **Tools and dashboards** (`body.tool`) switch the whole page to the sans at 14px. Keep the serif for any long explanatory text inside them.

If the environment allows hosted fonts and the reader asks, a display serif for headings with a text serif for body and a coding mono works well (for example Fraunces, Source Serif 4 and IBM Plex Mono). The local stacks are the default because they always load.

### 5.4 Space and density

- **4px grid.** All spacing uses the `--s1` to `--s9` tokens (4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32, 48, 64, 96px).
- **Sections breathe.** 64px above each `h2` in documents, 48px in tools. Never let a block of tiles or a table sit flush against the paragraph above it.
- **Page width.** Documents max out around 1180px including the contents rail; tools around 1440px.
- **Documents** are spacious. **Tools** are denser: 14px text, table rows about 32px tall, 8px list gaps, on the same grid.
- **One dense section in a reading page.** `body.doc` and `body.tool` set the whole page. When a page is mostly argument with one section of records the reader will work with, keep `body.doc` and wrap that section in `section.dataset`: it switches to tool density, may use the full content width, and caps its table in a scrolling frame (PATTERNS.md section 10).

### 5.5 Surfaces, borders, corners, shadows

- Prefer whitespace and a 1px hairline to a box.
- Cards (`.card`) only for real groups: a set of options, a set of people, a set of linked pages. **Never let two boxed things touch**; the gap is at least 16px.
- Corner radius 6px, 4px for buttons and inputs. Status and chip pills are fully rounded. Other large radii read as consumer app styling.
- Shadows are barely there (`--shadow`).
- Borders that only decorate use `--hairline`. Borders that outline a control use `--border`.

### 5.6 Ornament

One small recurring ornament per page at most, used no more than three times (`hr.mark`). No clip art, no icon per bullet, no emoji in headings or labels, no stock illustration.

## 6. Components and when to use them

Every component below is in `base.css` and shown in the component gallery at the end of `template.html`.

| Component | Use it for | Do not use it for |
|---|---|---|
| `.masthead` with `.kicker`, `h1`, `.dek`, `.meta` | Every page's opening | |
| `.bluf` | The bottom line of a page that argues or reports, once | Reference and tool pages (use `.purpose`) |
| `.purpose` | One sentence saying what a reference or tool page is for | Pages with a conclusion |
| `.stats` / `.stat` | Three to six headline numbers, each with what it is and its source | Numbers without a source; more than eight tiles |
| `.callout` (`.decision`, `.risk`, `.stop`) | One important aside per section at most | Ordinary paragraphs dressed up |
| `.status` | Any status (on track, at risk, blocked, idle) | Decoration |
| `.chip` | Small tags (Recommended, Draft, a team name) | Status |
| `.compare` / `.option.recommended` | Two to four options side by side | Long prose per option |
| `ol.steps` | Plans and processes | Unordered points |
| `ol.timeline` | Dated events | Overlapping work (use a table) |
| `.cards` / `.card` / `a.card` | Real groups of peers, link hubs | Wrapping prose |
| `.grid` | Peers that need no box; galleries of figures | Layout of a whole page |
| `.table-wrap` + `table` | Anything with rows and columns | Layout |
| `.table-wrap.scroll-y` | Tables over about 15 rows (header stays in view) | Short tables |
| `figure` + `.zoom-btn` + `.figure-frame` + `figcaption` | Every diagram, chart or image | Images with no caption |
| `.annotate` + `.pointer-note` | Explaining parts of a screen or figure | Decoration |
| `.term` + `dl.glossary` | Specialist terms | Common words |
| `details` | Detail some readers need | The ask, the risks, the main argument |
| `.tabs` | Parallel views of the same thing | Sequential content |
| `nav.toc` in `.with-toc` | Pages with four or more `h2`s or about 1,200 words | Short pages |
| `blockquote`, `.footnote`, `.sources` | Quotes with an attribution line, notes, the source list | |
| `pre`, `figure.code` | Code, with the file name as caption | Prose |
| `.field`, `label`, `input`, `select`, `textarea`, `fieldset`, `.check` | Tools, calculators and filters | |
| `th > button.sort` | Sortable columns | Tables of five rows or fewer |
| `.filter`, `.filter-sentence` | Narrowing a list; the sentence says what it shows | Lists of fewer than about 15 items |
| `section.dataset` | Records inside a reading page: filter, linked chart, sortable table | A whole page of records (use `body.tool`) |
| `.since` | What changed since the reader last looked (Monitor) | First-time readers' pages |

**Tables.** Wrap every table in `.table-wrap` so a wide table scrolls inside its own frame. Right-align numbers (`td.num`). Row headers are `th scope="row"`. For a comparison, put options in columns and criteria in rows, deciding rows first, and mark the recommended column with `class="rec"` on its cells. Keep `caption` to a short title. Put the source and date in `<p class="table-note">` after the wrapper, where it cannot scroll off a phone screen. A long table gets `.scroll-y` so its header stays in view. To build a table from data, use `tools/table.mjs`: it escapes every value, writes `data-sort` values, right-aligns numbers, renders status as a shape and a word, makes columns sortable and adds `.scroll-y` past 25 rows. With no shell, read the module and follow it by hand.

**Stat tiles.** The value large, then one line saying what it is, then the source and date in small grey. If a tile's value is a status, use a `.status` as the value.

**Tabs.** Use this markup. Author the panels without `hidden`; the script hides the inactive ones, and with script off every panel shows under its `.tab-label`.

```html
<div class="tabs">
  <div role="tablist" aria-label="Plan views">
    <button type="button" role="tab" id="tab-a" aria-controls="panel-a" aria-selected="true">By week</button>
    <button type="button" role="tab" id="tab-b" aria-controls="panel-b" aria-selected="false">By team</button>
  </div>
  <div role="tabpanel" id="panel-a" aria-labelledby="tab-a" tabindex="0"><p class="tab-label">By week</p>...</div>
  <div role="tabpanel" id="panel-b" aria-labelledby="tab-b" tabindex="0"><p class="tab-label">By team</p>...</div>
</div>
```

**Forms.** Labels sit above their control in a `.field`; controls are at least 40px tall. A checkbox goes inside `label.check`. Group related controls in a `fieldset` with a `legend`.

**Code.** Unhighlighted by default. If highlighting helps, wrap tokens in `.tok-k` (keywords), `.tok-s` (strings) and `.tok-c` (comments). Caption a code block with its file name (`figure.code`). Long lines scroll inside the block.

**Sorting.** A sortable column is `<th scope="col" data-col="days"><button type="button" class="sort">Days left</button></th>`. The template's script does the rest: one click sorts ascending, the next descending; it sets `aria-sort` on that `th` and `none` on the others; it sorts by the cell's `data-sort` when present, else its text, comparing numbers as numbers; ties keep their order; rows a filter hides stay hidden; and the order goes in the URL hash (`#sort=days.desc`). The indicator is a shape (both arrows, up, down) and hides in print. With script off the header text stays and the button does nothing. `check.mjs` fails a page that offers sort buttons with no script to sort.

**Empty states.** An empty list says so in one sentence and says what would fill it.

## 7. Diagrams, charts and images

This is where pages most often go wrong. Take it seriously.

### 7.1 Every figure must earn its place

A figure belongs on the page only if removing it would make the page harder to understand. Good reasons: a flow or sequence, what talks to what, where something sits in a larger whole, change over time, places relative to each other, the real thing (a photo of the site, a screenshot of the screen being discussed). Decorative diagrams, generic boxes that restate the paragraph, and text panels labelled as visuals all fail.

### 7.2 Legible at the size it is read

- **Every label is at least 12px at its displayed size.** Use `.d-label` (13 units) and `.d-small` (12 units) in diagrams, `.c-value` (13) and `.c-tick` (12) in charts.
- **Figures draw at 1:1 or larger, never smaller.** Give every figure SVG `style="--fig-min: Wpx"`, where W is its viewBox width. Then one viewBox unit is at least one CSS pixel, so a 12-unit label shows at 12px or more, and you can prove it by reading the code. On a screen narrower than W, the figure scrolls inside its frame and the page does not.
- **Figures grow to at most 1.25 times their size** on wide screens, so a small chart is never blown up. A small chart (viewBox up to about 400 wide) with `class="small-ok"` keeps exactly its own size. A chart that should fit a phone without scrolling is 320 units wide or less.
- **Every figure can be enlarged.** Put `<button class="zoom-btn">Enlarge</button>` first inside the `figure`, above `.figure-frame`, where it cannot cover the figure. The template's dialog opens the figure fitted to the window, with zoom in, zoom out and fit.
- No label overlaps a line, a box or another label. No text runs past the edge of its box or the viewBox. Estimate a label's width as characters times font size times 0.55 (0.6 for bold or uppercase), and keep at least 4 units of padding inside its box. `check.mjs` does this arithmetic for straight lines and boxes.

This rule is for figures drawn by hand or by `tools/bars.mjs`. A generated graph with more than about 40 nodes shows labels by their size on screen (semantic zoom) instead, and sits outside the 1:1 rule and `check.mjs` label arithmetic.

Arithmetic, for a reader with no browser: displayed size = label units x (rendered width / viewBox width), where rendered width = the larger of `--fig-min` and the column width, capped at 1.25 x `--fig-min`. The figure column is 324px on a 390px phone, about 622px at 720px, and about 798px on a desktop with the rail.

### 7.3 Inline SVG, with a machine-readable source

- Draw diagrams as inline SVG you write yourself, using the classes in `base.css` so they follow the theme. Give the SVG a `viewBox`, `role="img"`, a `<title>` and a `<desc>` that says in words what it shows, and `aria-labelledby` pointing at both.
- If you plan in Mermaid, render it to SVG yourself and inline the SVG; never load Mermaid. Keep the Mermaid text in `<script type="text/plain" class="diagram-source">` inside the `figure`. Never put it in an HTML comment: Mermaid's `-->` arrow ends the comment and the rest of the source shows on the page.
- Mermaid's automatic layout suits small flows (under about a dozen nodes). Wide flowcharts, highlighted paths across zones, ladders and rings come out cramped. Hand-place those, or split them into several figures.
- When several SVGs share a page, give each its own marker ids. Use `markerUnits="userSpaceOnUse"`. Give each figure two markers: a plain one, and one with `class="focus"` for accent edges. Their colour comes from CSS; never put `fill` on a marker path.

### 7.4 One idea per figure, and the caption says the idea

- Each figure makes one point. If it needs two, make two figures.
- The caption opens with a `<strong>` sentence stating the takeaway, then explains the encoding (what solid and dashed lines mean, what the colours are, with `.key.cat-N` swatches).
- Give figures stable ids (`FIG-1`, or `FIG-CACHE-PATH`) and refer to them by id in long pages.

### 7.5 Encoding rules

- **The focus is highlighted, the context is quiet.** The subject uses `.d-focus`; everything else uses `.d-box`.
- **Edge types by line style:** solid for data or the main flow, `.control` (long dash) for control or fallback, `.evidence` (dotted) for evidence or reference. Number the steps of a flow on the edges.
- **Zones and categories:** a zone or group boundary is `.d-zone` (dashed outline). Category nodes are `.d-cat-1` to `.d-cat-6`; solid chart marks are `.c-cat-1` to `.c-cat-6`. Same category, same number, everywhere on the page, with a legend.
- **Never write a colour in an SVG attribute.** Use the classes.
- **Data, estimates and illustrations are three different things.**
  - A figure with measured data names its source and date in the caption.
  - A figure with your own rough values marks them: the caption says "Illustrative" (`.mark-illustrative`), the SVG says it on its face (`text.d-mark`), and the values say "estimated".
  - A figure that shows only the shape of an idea says "Illustrative" and carries no values.

### 7.6 Annotating a figure or a screen

- Show the subject large, nearly the full width.
- **Dim everything except the part being explained** (`.annotate .context`), so the subject (`.annotate .subject`) stands out at full contrast.
- Use **pointer callouts** (`.pointer-note` with `.up`, `.down`, `.left` or `.right`), numbered to match the explanation. One number per thing.

### 7.7 Charts

- Pick the form by the question: change over time is a line, comparison across categories is a sorted bar, part of a whole with two or three parts is a single stacked bar, distribution is a histogram. Avoid pies with more than three slices, 3D, dual axes, and donuts with a number in the middle.
- Erase what carries no data. Gridlines, if any, are `.c-grid` and faint. Label bars and lines directly (`.c-value`, which has a halo so a gridline never cuts through it) instead of a legend where you can.
- Classes: `.c-bar` (neutral), `.c-bar.focus` (accent), `.c-cat-N` (series), `.c-line`, `.c-axis`, `.c-grid`, `.c-tick`, `.c-value`.
- **Compute every coordinate from the data**, in a short script if you have a shell, and write the scale in a comment beside the SVG (`<!-- x = 96 + minutes * 22 -->`) so a reader can check any bar. Hand-placed bar lengths are where generated charts most often go wrong.
- Put the numbers in the SVG's `<desc>`. A chart with five or more values also gets a collapsed table under it.
- Hand-write charts as SVG, or generate them with `tools/bars.mjs` when you have a shell: it sorts the bars, sizes the label column with the same width estimate `check.mjs` uses, picks a round axis, places each value inside or outside its bar, writes the scale comment and returns the whole `figure`. With no shell, read the module and follow the same arithmetic by hand, and write it in the handover. No charting library.
- **Records and the chart that filters them.** Once a page has more than about 25 records, show an aggregate chart and the records table it filters (`section.dataset`, PATTERNS.md section 10). The chart is an aggregate, so it keeps direct labels and counts. Its bars are buttons: picking one filters the table and the sentence above it, picking it again clears it. Each bar counts the records under every filter except its own facet, so the bars stay a way to switch. Never turn records into prose, and never paste them into a reading page as one long static table.

### 7.8 Photos and images

- Use real photos when they show the real thing. Say so in the caption when an image is generated. Never use stock imagery as decoration.
- Images are `data:` URIs inside the one file, so budget them: resize to at most 800px on the long edge, JPEG or WebP at about quality 70, roughly 60 to 120KB each. Keep the page under 2MB.
- Every `img` has `alt`. A gallery is a `.grid` of figures, each with a caption.

### 7.9 Maps

A page cannot load map tiles or a map library. A map is either a schematic inline SVG (relative positions, labelled, marked "Illustrative") or a static image you were given. Link each place to an external map app by address.

## 8. Interaction

Everything interactive is a few lines of vanilla JavaScript in the page. No frameworks, no libraries, no build step, no network.

Worth having:

- The contents rail with the current section marked (template).
- Figure enlarge with zoom (template).
- Collapsible detail (`<details>`, no script needed).
- Hover or focus definitions for terms (CSS only).
- Tabs for parallel views, with arrow keys (template).
- Sortable columns, filters with a sentence saying what the list shows, and a chart that filters the table under it (template; PATTERNS.md section 13).
- Click-to-reveal for self-test content (a `details` block works).
- A three-state theme switch (auto, light, dark) and palette switch, for tools the reader will live in.
- Deep links to every section and every object.

Rules:

- Motion is short (150 to 200ms), shows where something went, and is off when the reader's system asks for reduced motion.
- No auto-playing anything, no parallax, no scroll-jacking, no animated counters, no confetti.
- The page reads correctly with every section closed and with JavaScript off. Put `class="no-js"` on `html`; the head script removes it, and `base.css` hides script-only controls while it is there.
- Wrap every `localStorage` call in `try`/`catch`, key it to the page (`location.pathname`), and never let a stored choice override a `data-theme` written in the markup. Use storage only for conveniences like the last theme and for a reader's own progress through a Do page. A view someone might share, such as a filter, lives in the URL hash instead (PATTERNS.md section 9).
- Filter, sort and chart changes push browser history, so Back undoes them, and in-page links keep the filter (PATTERNS.md section 9).
- Script that builds content from text uses `textContent`, never `innerHTML`.

## 9. Responsive and print

### 9.1 Phones

- **The page never scrolls sideways.** At 390px wide, `document.documentElement.scrollWidth` equals the window width. Wide tables and figures scroll inside their own frames, with a shadow at the edge that has more content.
- 16px side gutter on phones, 32px from 720px up.
- Grids collapse with `minmax(min(100%, Npx), 1fr)`; stat tiles go two to a row. Grid children have `min-width: 0` so a long value wraps instead of widening the page.
- No fixed width over 358px (390 minus the gutters) outside a scrolling frame.
- The bottom line comes before the contents rail; the rail is a collapsed box.
- Documents read over must be comfortable on a phone. Tools may be desktop-first, but must not break.

### 9.2 Print

What a printed copy is for depends on the pattern: a signed-off decision, a dated snapshot, a paper checklist. PATTERNS.md section 12 and each pattern's "Print and PDF" entry say what changes. The rules below hold for every page.

- Printing uses the light palette regardless of the screen theme, white background, 11pt text. Scope every dark rule to `@media screen`; an unscoped dark rule prints pale text on dark boxes from any machine set to dark mode.
- The contents rail, toolbar and enlarge buttons hide. Collapsed sections and inactive tab panels print open.
- Headings and glossary terms never sit alone at the bottom of a page; figures, cards, callouts and table rows do not split. Expect some blank space at page ends as a result.
- Never use `position: fixed` for print furniture; use the browser's print header and footer.
- Check that wide figures are still legible at printed size; split them if not.

## 10. Technical constraints

- **One file.** All CSS in a `<style>` tag, all JavaScript in `<script>` tags, images as inline SVG or `data:` URIs. It opens correctly from the file system with no network.
- **No external anything:** no CDNs, web fonts, analytics, icon fonts, Mermaid, Chart.js, D3, Tailwind, React or jQuery.
- **Several pages** are several self-contained files that link to each other by relative path, each with the same masthead and a link back to an index page.
- **Inline `base.css` in full,** then add page CSS after its end marker, using the tokens.
- Semantic HTML: `header`, `nav`, `main`, `section`, `figure`, `figcaption`, `table` with `thead`, real `button`s for actions.
- Keep the page under 2MB including images.
- Accessibility floor: WCAG AA contrast, visible keyboard focus, `title`/`desc` on every figure and `alt` on every image, headings in order. Pages shared with others keep the full floor.

## 11. What makes a page look bad

Avoid all of these. Most are the default look of machine-generated pages.

- A centred hero with a big gradient headline, then a grid of identical feature cards with an emoji or icon each.
- Purple, violet or blue-to-pink gradients; glassmorphism; glowing shadows; neon accents.
- Everything wrapped in a rounded card with a shadow, cards nested in cards, cards touching.
- Inter or another default sans for long reading text.
- ALL CAPS headings, or tiny uppercase labels on everything.
- Emoji in headings, labels or bullets.
- A different colour for every section, badge and button.
- Walls of bullets, or bullets three levels deep. Paragraphs carry reasoning; bullets carry lists.
- Topic-label headings in the argument ("Overview", "Key Insights", "Conclusion").
- A summary at the end that repeats the page, instead of the answer at the start.
- Tiny diagrams, especially auto-laid-out Mermaid shrunk to fit, with labels too small to read and no way to zoom.
- Diagrams with labels on lines, text running out of boxes, or a legend listing distinctions the drawing never shows.
- Decorative images and stock illustrations.
- Horizontal scrolling on a phone.
- A dark-only design, or a light design that breaks in dark mode.
- Jargon and internal codes used as names without definition.
- Numbers with no source.
- Text-only "visual" panels.
- Busy annotation where the callouts and the subject compete at the same contrast.
- Animations, counters, auto-advancing carousels.

## 12. Verify before you hand it over

"It should look fine" is not evidence. Use the highest tier you have, and say which.

**Every page, every tier: verified by reading.** Run `node check.mjs page.html` (or `bun`, or `deno run --allow-read`) if you have a shell. It reads the file, needs no browser, and prints PASS, FAIL and WARN lines with line numbers. With no shell, do the same checks by reading the file (CHECKLIST "Verified by reading"), and write the label-size arithmetic for each figure (section 7.2) into your handover. Fix every FAIL, or explain it.

**Tier 1, a browser is available: verified by looking.**

1. **Render it.** In headless Chrome, `--window-size` is unreliable below about 500px. Use the DevTools protocol (`Emulation.setDeviceMetricsOverride` with `mobile: true`) for a true 390px phone.
2. **Capture four views:** desktop around 1280 to 1440px, phone at 390px, dark mode, and print preview.
3. **Look at each region at full size,** never only a scaled-down full-page shot. Zoom into every figure. Check for labels on lines, clipped text, cards touching, single words alone on a heading's last line, and anything flush against something else.
4. **Measure sideways scroll** at 390px, including with a `.term` focused.
5. **Click through** the contents rail, every enlarge button, every tab, every collapsible.
6. **Fix, then look again.**

**Tier 2, no browser.** Do the reading checks, fix what fails, and hand over with: "Not rendered. Checked by reading: [list]. Please look at: [the two or three things only a render can confirm]." Never write "I looked at it" or "it renders correctly" at Tier 2.

**Replacing or restyling a page that already exists.** Run `node check.mjs new.html --was old.html`. It lists the interactions each page offers (sort, filter, linked chart, tabs, zoom, rail, collapsibles, terms, checklist progress, print button, theme) and fails for each one the new page drops, and it compares table row counts. With no shell, list the old page's interactions by reading it. Either way, name in the handover every interaction kept and every one dropped.

**Generated pieces.** Check each generated figure or table on its own first with `node check.mjs --fragment piece.html`, then the whole page. What a static check cannot settle: which fonts the reader's machine has, collisions on curved or diagonal lines, the scroll shadow, print page breaks, the zoom dialog, and how the page feels at a glance. Name the ones that matter for this page.

**Cold read, every tier.** Read the bottom line and the headings only. Do they tell the story? Then read as an outsider: any sentence you would need to read twice gets rewritten, any undefined term gets defined.

## 13. Page recipes, by pattern

Each recipe is a pattern from `PATTERNS.md` with the details a common request adds. Choose the pattern first, then use the recipe. Each pattern has a working page in `examples/`.

**Decision brief** (Decide; `examples/decide.html`, and the brief in `template.html`). Masthead, bottom line (ask, why now, cost, stop conditions), situation with stat tiles, the model or design with one figure, options compared with the recommendation marked, the plan as steps, main risk callout, then the annex: detail in collapsibles, glossary, sources. Two printed pages for the top part.

**Comparison** (Decide). Bottom line with the recommendation, one comparison table (options as columns, criteria as rows, deciding rows first, recommended column marked), then a short section per option on what the table cannot show.

**Research report** (Review when it reports findings, `examples/review.html`; Understand when it explains a field, `examples/understand.html`). Masthead, bottom line (finding, confidence, implication), the key findings as claim headings each with its evidence, a comparison table if there are alternatives, a section on what is still unknown (a claim heading such as "Two questions stay open until the pilot ends"), then the annex: method, glossary, sources. From the template, drop the stat tiles and the steps if there is no plan.

**Explainer or learning guide** (Understand; `examples/understand.html`). Masthead, a `.purpose` sentence saying what the reader will understand by the end, the big-picture figure first, then sections in the order the concepts depend on each other, each with a worked example and a figure where one earns its place, a section on common misreadings, a self-check with click-to-reveal answers, glossary.

**Status or dashboard** (Monitor; `examples/monitor.html`). `body.tool`, `graphite` or `slate` palette. One computed status sentence in `.bluf` with an "as of" time, four tiles each answering one question, the exceptions before anything that is fine, the working list with filters kept in the URL hash, then the trend. `.status` everywhere, never colour alone.

**Field guide for use on the move** (Do; `examples/do.html`). Travel, an event, a site visit. Phone-first. The plan for the day as numbered steps, then one card per place with a small photo (see 7.8 for the budget), the address as a link to a map app, the time, and what to do there. Large tap targets, nothing that needs a laptop. A packing list is the same pattern: one item per step, with ticks kept on the reader's phone.

**Reference page** (Look up; `examples/look-up.html`). Glossary, lookup table, catalogue. A `.purpose` sentence, a filter once there are more than about 15 entries, an index, then entries that all have the same shape. No `.bluf`.
